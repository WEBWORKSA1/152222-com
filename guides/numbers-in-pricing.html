Why prices end in 9

The left-digit effect

We read numbers left to right and anchor on the first digit. $2.99 feels closer to $2 than to $3, even though it is one cent from $3. Researchers call this the left-digit effect, and prices ending in 9, 99 or 95 are known as charm prices. The effect is strongest when the left digit actually changes — 2.99 vs 3.00 — and much weaker when it doesn't (3.59 vs 3.60).

What the evidence says

The best-known field test comes from economists Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester, who ran experiments with a mail-order clothing catalogue in which the same dress was offered at $34, $39 and $44 in different versions of the catalogue. The $39 version sold more than both the cheaper $34 and the dearer $44 versions. Their later review in the Harvard Business Review argued that 9-endings also act as a signal — shoppers have learned to read them as "this is a deal".

A study of millions of used-car auction sales (Lacetera, Pope and Sydnor) found prices drop in discrete steps as odometer readings cross each 10,000-mile mark — a car just under the threshold fetches noticeably more than one just over it. It's another left-digit effect, this time on a quantity rather than a price.

When round prices win

Charm prices say "bargain". That is exactly wrong for luxury, gifts and premium services, where buyers want to feel good about spending. Round prices (₹5,000, $200) are processed more fluently and feel more "right" for emotional purchases. That's why fine-dining menus often drop currency symbols and decimals altogether, and why consultants quote $10,000 rather than $9,999.

Precise numbers in negotiation

The opposite trick applies in negotiation. Experiments on first offers found that a precise anchor ($4,850) pulls counter-offers closer than a round one ($5,000), because a precise number implies careful research. In real-estate listings, precise asking prices have been associated with final prices nearer the ask.

Culture on the price tag

  • India: ₹99, ₹499 and ₹999 are standard e-commerce price points. For gifts and ceremonies the logic flips: amounts like ₹101, ₹501 and ₹1,001 add a "shagun" 1 so the gift doesn't end on zero.
  • China: 8 sounds like the word for prosperity, so prices ending in 8 (¥88, ¥168, ¥888) are common, and 4 (which sounds like "death") is avoided.
  • Japan: 4 and 9 are avoided in gift sets and room numbers for similar sound-alike reasons.

Using this in your business

  1. Discount or volume products: charm prices that change the left digit (₹999, not ₹949).
  2. Premium or gifting: round prices.
  3. B2B quotes and negotiations: precise numbers backed by real line items.
  4. Selling into specific cultures: check the digits your buyers love and avoid.
  5. Test it. The effect size varies by product and audience. A/B test two price endings before rolling one out.
